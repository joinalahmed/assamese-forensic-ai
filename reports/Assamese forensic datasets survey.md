# Gated AI4Bharat corpora anchor Assamese forensic AI

No public dataset matches the forensic conditions of any of the four experiments, but a small set of gated-yet-free resources moves three of them decisively forward and should be adopted before anything is collected. For speaker comparison (E3), **IndicVoices** is the single highest-value acquisition: CC BY 4.0, roughly **850 Assamese speakers** with speaker IDs, and the only openly licensed Assamese speech recorded over an **8 kHz telephony bridge** — a direct replacement for five InfoBay sample calls and the sole route to in-domain fine-tuning of the Mandarin-trained encoders. For machine-text detection (E4), no multilingual MGT benchmark contains Assamese or Bengali, so the win is to broaden the machine class locally: **Sangraha Synthetic's 11.7 billion IndicTrans2-translated Assamese tokens** are genre-matched to the project's own Wikipedia negatives, and **Qwen3** (Apache 2.0) is the one open LLM that lists Assamese by name. For back-transliteration (E2), **Aksharantar-asm (179K pairs) and IndicXlit** widen lexical coverage while **Sangraha-verified (292M tokens) plus IndicCorp v2 (3.38M lines, CC0)** supply the native-script LM the sentence-level reranker needs; the project should also note that its core AssameseBackTranslit repository still carries no licence. Authorship verification (E1) is the experiment with no good public answer: the only author-labelled Assamese text is **AAALC (16 literary authors, CC BY 4.0)**, and the only ≥100-author, multi-session Assamese source is the **Assamese Wikipedia revision history**, which is register-mismatched. Short informal multi-author Assamese text, multi-session Assamese telephone speech and any Assamese spoofed audio do not exist publicly and must be built. The researchers worked without a web-search tool; paywalled Springer/ACM items, dead IIT Guwahati pages and several gated dataset schemas remain unverified and are flagged throughout.

## Forensic-condition Assamese is the scarce input, not Assamese per se

The survey found **84 Assamese-tagged datasets on Hugging Face** ([HF datasets API](https://huggingface.co/api/datasets?search=assamese&limit=200)), hundreds of millions of native-script tokens, and tens of thousands of hours of Assamese audio. Yet almost none of it carries the metadata forensic science requires: per-author or per-speaker identity, multiple sessions per individual, a telephone channel, or genuine informal register. Social-media Assamese collections exist but are released without user identity, speech corpora are single-visit, and no machine-generated-text benchmark has ever included the language. That is why the report sorts every resource into three tiers: **download today** (open licence, no or automatic gate), **request or paid** (manual approval, undertaking, enterprise licence, or challenge registration), and **must be collected** (no public resource satisfies the condition). The gate matters less than it sounds for the AI4Bharat and IISc corpora, which are CC BY 4.0 behind a click-through, but it matters a great deal for LDC-IL, InfoBay and LDC, where terms and prices were not retrievable.

A second cross-cutting finding is that **Bengali is the natural transfer language** but is not a free substitute. Bengali and Assamese share a script, differing in ৰ/ৱ and in romanisation habits (Assamese /x/ as "xubho", for instance), and the notes document Bengali contamination as a known failure mode of LLM Assamese output ([HF promptmafiainc](https://huggingface.co/datasets/promptmafiainc/assamese-bengali-contrastive-eval)). Bengali resources therefore serve as pre-training and cohort data, never as evaluation data for Assamese claims.

## E1: Wikipedia revision history is the only ≥100-author Assamese source

The project's Arambhani poetry corpus shares the central weakness of every author-labelled Assamese resource found: literary register and few authors. The single openly downloadable author-labelled Assamese corpus is **AAALC** on Mendeley Data (DOI 10.17632/m24w2n2jtb.3, version 3, 16 Sep 2024): **16 "prominent poets, writers, and novelists"**, novels, articles and short stories assembled by scraping and OCR, released **CC BY 4.0** with an open "Download All" button; the record gives no document or word counts ([Mendeley Data](https://data.mendeley.com/datasets/m24w2n2jtb/3)). The researchers infer that AAALC is the same family as the "9,730-sample / 16-author" corpus described in two paywalled Springer chapters, but could not open those chapters, so the identification is unconfirmed ([Bing results index](https://www.bing.com/search?q=Assamese+authorship+attribution+corpus+dataset+16+authors)). AAALC is worth adopting immediately as a second closed-set literary benchmark and, more usefully, as a cross-register probe: a verifier trained on poetry and tested on AAALC prose exposes genre dependence before any claim about informal text is made.

For the actual target — short, informal, many authors, several sessions each — the only Assamese source that satisfies the author-count and session requirement is the **Assamese Wikipedia full-history dump**. The 01 Oct 2026 dumps include `aswiki-latest-pages-meta-history.xml.bz2` (~390 MB), a 7z variant (~170 MB) and a **~51 MB stub-history file** holding revision metadata only ([Wikimedia dumps](https://dumps.wikimedia.org/aswiki/latest/)); the wiki has **26,021 articles, 128,389 pages and 648,410 edits** ([as.wikipedia Special:Statistics](https://as.wikipedia.org/wiki/Special:Statistics)). Every revision names an account or IP with a timestamp, so diffs can be grouped into sessions per editor, and the text is CC BY-SA, which permits redistributing derived author-labelled splits — a licensing position no Facebook- or WhatsApp-derived corpus can offer. The cost is register: encyclopedic prose, with talk and user-talk namespaces as the most informal slice. The cheap first step is to parse the 51 MB stub file to count accounts with enough revisions before downloading the full history; per-author volume is currently unknown.

Genuinely informal Assamese exists but without authors. **AssameseBackTranslit** holds **60,312 Roman–native sentence pairs** from Facebook, Twitter and YouTube ([ACL Anthology](https://aclanthology.org/2024.lrec-main.143/)), and its paper's source table shows the material comes from a handful of pages and channels (GU Confession Page, CMO Assam, Dimpu's Vlogs, News Live, @himantabiswa), from **409,168 Facebook, 385,676 YouTube and 285,676 Twitter posts collected** of which 5,300 / 50,000 / 5,012 were annotated ([ACL Anthology PDF](https://aclanthology.org/2024.lrec-main.143.pdf)). The released files carry no user IDs, but the raw collection evidently does, which makes the IIT Guwahati OSINTG group the one party to approach for a consented, user-ID-preserving release; their resources page returned HTTP 404 on 31 Oct 2026 ([Bing results index](https://www.bing.com/search?q=IIT+Guwahati+OSINT+Assamese+Facebook+Twitter+YouTube+dataset+github)). **AS-EMO** offers **4,502 Facebook/WhatsApp/YouTube posts** in native, Roman and code-mixed Assamese under an MIT repository licence, with an unretrievable privacy section and no author field ([GitHub repo](https://github.com/ponpritomboruah/Assamese-Code-Mixed-Emotion-Classification)); the 26,444-word Kalita & Saharia Facebook corpus has no licence at all ([GitHub repo](https://github.com/nayanjk12/assamese-english-codemixed-dataset)). These are domain-adaptive pre-training material for a short-text Assamese encoder, not verification training data. The **IndicXNLI triplet sets** (e.g. `KhyontekAI/Assamese-IndicXNLI-Triplet-Random-Negatives`, CC0, 1M–10M rows) are the obvious contrastive warm-start for a siamese verifier ([HF datasets API](https://huggingface.co/api/datasets?search=assamese&limit=100)).

For transfer, **BAAD16** gives **16 Bangla authors, 17,966 samples and 13.4M+ words**, publicly on Mendeley Data, with an accompanying ULMFiT paper reporting 99.8% attribution accuracy ([arXiv abstract](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.05519)); the exact Mendeley DOI was not retrieved. It reproduces AAALC's register limits and is useful for representation learning in the shared script, not for open-set calibration. Hindi–English code-mixed Twitter corpora from IIIT Hyderabad exist for Roman-script register transfer but none is described as user-labelled ([Bing results index](https://www.bing.com/search?q=code-mixed+Hindi-English+authorship+attribution+dataset+tweets+user+ids)). PAN cross-domain authorship data, FIRE/HASOC code-mixed tracks and the Blog Authorship Corpus were not reached in this session.

Priority for E1: first, parse the aswiki stub history and build an open-set split over ≥100 editors with session-separated impostor trials, concentrating on talk namespaces; second, add AAALC as a second literary set and run poetry→prose cross-register tests; third, pre-train the encoder on AssameseBackTranslit, AS-EMO and the IndicXNLI triplets; fourth, open a request to IIT Guwahati for a consented user-ID release of the OSINTG collection. The experiment the project actually wants — informal short messages from ≥100 known authors — has to be collected under an ethics regime, and nothing located changes that.

## E2: Aksharantar and a native-script LM attack the ambiguity ceiling

The project already uses the best Assamese resource in existence for this task. The notes add detail that matters: the OSINTG repository `osintg-iitghy/LREC-COLING-2024-OSINTG-IITG` ships `sentence-aligned.txt` (**120,628 lines → 60,314 pairs**), `word-aligned.txt` (**527,642 token-level pairs**, with repeats) and 70/10/20 word splits of 45,934 / 6,560 / 13,120, and its README states that licensing "will be updated after the conference proceedings" — **no licence file exists as of 31 Oct 2026** ([GitHub repo](https://github.com/osintg-iitghy/LREC-COLING-2024-OSINTG-IITG)). The paper reports mean sentence length 11.14 words, 20.1% code-mixing, **79,200 unique Assamese words, 65,614 transliteration pairs, up to 127 Roman variants per native word and up to 31 native words per Roman form**, with inter-annotator κ 0.83 ([ACL Anthology PDF](https://aclanthology.org/2024.lrec-main.143.pdf)). That last statistic is the ceiling the project has hit: word-level models cannot resolve a 31-way ambiguity, and only sentence context can.

The only other sizeable Assamese Roman↔native resource is **Aksharantar** (AI4Bharat): **179K train / 4K val / 5,531 test Assamese pairs**, licensed CC-BY for the manually collected portion and CC0 for mined portions ([Hugging Face dataset card](https://huggingface.co/datasets/ai4bharat/Aksharantar)). Its composition is the caveat: of ~217K unique Assamese pairs, **203K are mined from IndicCorp** news text and 19K are annotator-produced forward romanisations, not organic social-media spellings ([arXiv 2205.03018 PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2205.03018)). The register mismatch is measured, not speculative: **IndicXlit zero-shot scores 73.38 WER on AssameseBackTranslit against 55.05 for the in-domain Transformer**, and even fine-tuning IndicXlit on the project's data (63.53 WER) underperforms training from scratch ([ACL Anthology PDF](https://aclanthology.org/2024.lrec-main.143.pdf)). Aksharantar's value is therefore coverage of rare words and named entities, and **IndicXlit** (MIT, 21 languages including Assamese, ≈11M parameters; CTranslate2 repack at ~1 ms/word) is best used as an n-best candidate generator feeding a reranker rather than as the primary model ([GitHub AI4Bharat/IndicXlit](https://github.com/AI4Bharat/IndicXlit); [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/Singla0009/all-indic-transliteration)). Google's Dakshina omits Assamese entirely ([GitHub dakshina](https://github.com/google-research-datasets/dakshina)), and the same group's ACM TALLIP 2024 paper is closed access with no release statement ([OpenAlex record](https://api.openalex.org/works/doi:10.1145/3639565)).

The reranker needs a native-script word-level LM, and here the open supply is ample. **Sangraha** offers **292.1M human-verified Assamese tokens** (plus 17.5M unverified) under CC BY 4.0, ungated ([HF ai4bharat/sangraha](https://huggingface.co/datasets/ai4bharat/sangraha)); **IndicCorp v2** adds **3.38M Assamese lines under CC0** ([HF ai4bharat/IndicCorpV2](https://huggingface.co/datasets/ai4bharat/IndicCorpV2)); **MWirelabs** provides **1.61M sentences / 77.4M tokens** under CC BY-SA 4.0 but overlaps Samanantar and IndicCorp ([HF](https://huggingface.co/datasets/MWirelabs/assamese-monolingual-corpus)). All are news-heavy, so the native side of the project's own sentence file (589,289 Assamese tokens) remains the domain-adaptation layer. Sangraha's **11.7B-token synthetic split includes `asm_Latn`**, IndicXlit-romanised translated Wikipedia — the only billion-scale romanised Assamese anywhere, suitable for pre-training a romanised encoder but reflecting canonical rather than user spellings ([HF ai4bharat/sangraha](https://huggingface.co/datasets/ai4bharat/sangraha)). For an independent held-out test, **Bhasha-Abhijnaanam** supplies **512 romanised plus 1,012 native Assamese sentences** with parallel pairs, CC0 ([Hugging Face dataset card](https://huggingface.co/datasets/ai4bharat/Bhasha-Abhijnaanam)); it is small but it is the only Assamese sentence-level test set not produced by the project's own annotators.

Bengali transfer is rich. **BanglaTLit** (Findings of EMNLP 2024) releases **42.7K romanised↔Bangla social-media pairs and a 245.7K romanised pretraining corpus** via the Anthology attachment and GitHub, licence unstated ([ACL Anthology](https://aclanthology.org/2024.findings-emnlp.859/)); **BanglaMix** gives **12,576 YouTube comments with 120,866 token-level LID tags and Bengali-script transliterations under CC-BY** on Zenodo ([OpenAlex](https://api.openalex.org/works/doi:10.1016/j.dib.2026.113317)); **Dakshina-bn** has a 25K-word lexicon and **10,000 manually romanised Wikipedia sentences under CC BY-SA 4.0** ([GitHub dakshina](https://github.com/google-research-datasets/dakshina)); CC-100 provides **164 MB of `bn_rom`** but no `as_rom` ([data.statmt.org/cc-100](https://data.statmt.org/cc-100/)). Bangladeshi romanisation conventions differ from Assamese ones, so these initialise rather than supervise. BanglaMix is also the closest thing to token-level LID gold in the script family: no Assamese–English–Hindi word-level LID set is public, the IALP 2018 Facebook corpus was never released ([OpenAlex](https://api.openalex.org/works/doi:10.1109/ialp.2018.8629104)), and ACE-Mix gives no size ([OpenAlex](https://api.openalex.org/works?search=ACE-Mix%20Assamese-English%20code-mixed&per-page=3)). The project can derive a "keep Latin / transliterate" token label from its own sentence file, where English stays in Latin script, but the observed sample shows Hindi tokens handled inconsistently, so that signal needs auditing first ([GitHub raw file](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/osintg-iitghy/LREC-COLING-2024-OSINTG-IITG/main/sentence-aligned.txt)).

Priority for E2: first, train a native-script word LM on Sangraha-verified plus IndicCorp v2, adapted on the project's native sentences, and use it to rerank IndicXlit n-best candidates — the direct attack on the 31-way ambiguity; second, merge Aksharantar-asm into training for vocabulary coverage while monitoring social-media WER; third, hold out Bhasha-Abhijnaanam as an external test; fourth, pre-train the romanised encoder on BanglaTLit-PT and Sangraha `asm_Latn`. In parallel, ask OSINTG to post the promised licence, since every downstream artefact of E2 inherits that uncertainty.

## E3: IndicVoices supplies 850 speakers and the only open 8 kHz Assamese

Five InfoBay calls scored by Mandarin-trained encoders is a zero-shot demonstration, not a calibrated system. The data to change that is **IndicVoices** (AI4Bharat): **23.7K hours from 51K speakers across 22 languages**, CC BY 4.0, gated by a click-through on Hugging Face, with per-audio JSON metadata ([HF ai4bharat/IndicVoices](https://huggingface.co/datasets/ai4bharat/IndicVoices)). The v1 paper's Assamese split is **684 training speakers / 100 h and 163 test speakers / 5 h**, recorded on participants' own varied phones at 44.1 kHz for solo tasks and — critically — **over an 8 kHz cloud telephony bridge for two-party role-play conversations**, with released metadata described as district ID, speaker ID and language ID and no PII ([arXiv 2403.01926 HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01926v1)). Each participant recorded in one 1–4 hour visit, so sessions are not multiplied, but task and channel diversity within a visit allow within-speaker cross-channel trials (44.1 kHz solo versus 8 kHz conversation) if speaker IDs are consistent across tasks — a point the card does not confirm. **IndicVoices-R**, the enhanced 48 kHz derivative (1,704 h, 10,496 speakers, CC BY 4.0), explicitly lists "speaker ID, gender, age group, duration, and language" per entry ([HF ai4bharat/indicvoices_r](https://huggingface.co/datasets/ai4bharat/indicvoices_r)), which strongly implies the parent carries the same fields.

**VAANI** (IISc/ARTPARK) is the cohort pool: **786 h from 4,217 speakers in Assam, of which 387 h are Assamese**, district-labelled (Kamrup Metropolitan, Sonitpur, Hailakandi, Karbi Anglong), CC BY 4.0, gated, **7.21 TB** on Hugging Face with no documented per-row schema or sampling rate ([vaani.iisc.ac.in](https://vaani.iisc.ac.in/); [HF ARTPARK-IISc/Vaani](https://huggingface.co/datasets/ARTPARK-IISc/Vaani)). It also covers Nagamese (296 h in Nagaland), Karbi and Sylheti as separate labels, useful for a non-Assamese impostor cohort from the same region. **LDC-IL's Assamese Raw Speech Corpus** is the one dialect-labelled source: **54 h, 304 speakers (154 F / 150 M), four regions — Xiboxagoria, Central Assam, Kamrupi, Goalparia** — at 48 kHz, free to recognised Indian academic institutions under a Non-Commercial Undertaking and priced in undisclosed tiers otherwise, delivered on physical media posted from Mysuru ([LDC-IL Assamese Raw Speech Corpus](https://data.ldcil.org/speech/speech-raw-corpus/assamese-raw-speech-corpus); [LDC-IL FAQ](https://data.ldcil.org/faq)). It suits dialect profiling, not telephone calibration. Resources to skip for speaker work: **SPRING-INX Assamese** (29,530 conversational clips with heavy code-switching but **no speaker column**) ([HF SPRINGLab/SPRING_INX_Assamese_R1](https://huggingface.co/datasets/SPRINGLab/SPRING_INX_Assamese_R1)), Kathbath and Shrutilipi (no Assamese), and Common Voice Assamese, which has moved to the Mozilla Data Collective and could not be inventoried ([HF common_voice_17_0](https://huggingface.co/datasets/mozilla-foundation/common_voice_17_0)). The **InfoBay** full catalogue claims 58,478 Assamese call-centre hours at 8 kHz dual-channel, but the schema has **no demographic or speaker-ID fields**, no transcripts, and access is by undisclosed enterprise licence ([HF InfoBayAI dual channel](https://huggingface.co/datasets/InfoBayAI/Assamese_Call_Center_Audio_Dataset_Dual_Channel)) — more of the same channel, without the metadata that would make it forensic training data.

For transfer and benchmarking, **Kathbath/IndicSUPERB** is the only Indic ASV benchmark with trial lists: 12 languages without Assamese, **Bengali 46 speakers**, speaker and gender encoded in filenames, and x-vector EERs of **2–3% on known speakers but 11–15% on unseen speakers** ([GitHub AI4Bharat/IndicSUPERB](https://github.com/AI4Bharat/IndicSUPERB); [arXiv 2208.11761 HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2208.11761v2)). That unseen-speaker band brackets the project's own zero-shot 2–10% on Assamese and says Indic mobile speech is simply hard for VoxCeleb-style encoders without adaptation. **I-MSV 2022** (100 speakers, 155 h, **multi-session, five sensors**, device-mismatched tests) is the closest multi-session Indic set but access is by challenge registration and languages are unlisted ([arXiv 2211.16694 HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2211.16694v2)). Telephone-band multilingual trials exist only as paid LDC corpora such as **SRE16** (220 speakers × 10 calls, Tagalog/Cantonese/Cebuano/Mandarin) ([LDC2019S20](https://catalog.ldc.upenn.edu/LDC2019S20)). Among encoders, no checkpoint trained on any Indic corpus was found; WeSpeaker's **VoxBlink2 SimAMResNet** models are the only zoo entries labelled multilingual and are the obvious extra zero-shot candidates ([WeSpeaker pretrained.md](https://github.com/wenet-e2e/wespeaker/blob/master/docs/pretrained.md)), while 3D-Speaker's CAM++/ERes2Net models are trained on 200k Mandarin speakers with "no Indic or broader multilingual training data" ([GitHub 3D-Speaker](https://github.com/modelscope/3D-Speaker)).

Anti-spoofing is the clearest "must be collected" case: **no spoof corpus contains Assamese**. **IndicSynth** covers 12 Indic languages (Bengali, not Assamese) with 4,000+ hours from XTTS-v2, VITS and FreeVC24 on Kathbath speakers, CC BY-NC 4.0 "academic research only" ([HF vdivyasharma/IndicSynth](https://huggingface.co/datasets/vdivyasharma/IndicSynth)); **MLAAD v11** has 1,153.6 h across 54 languages including **Bangla 14.1 h and Hindi 22.5 h** but no Assamese and no South-Asian bona-fide audio, also CC BY-NC ([arXiv 2401.09512 HTML v11](https://arxiv.org/html/2401.09512v11)); SEA-Spoof covers Tamil and Hindi and reports "severe cross-lingual degradation" that in-language fine-tuning largely recovers ([arXiv 2509.19865](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.19865)). The generators to build an Assamese spoof set exist today: **Indic Parler-TTS** (Apache 2.0, four Assamese voices) ([HF ai4bharat/indic-parler-tts](https://huggingface.co/ai4bharat/indic-parler-tts)), **IndicF5** zero-shot voice cloning with Assamese (MIT, gated, clone-with-permission condition) ([HF ai4bharat/IndicF5](https://huggingface.co/ai4bharat/IndicF5)), **MMS-TTS asm** (CC-BY-NC 4.0) ([HF facebook/mms-tts-asm](https://huggingface.co/facebook/mms-tts-asm)), and language-agnostic FreeVC-style conversion; XTTS-v2 does not support Assamese or Bengali ([HF coqui/XTTS-v2](https://huggingface.co/coqui/XTTS-v2)). The detector warm-start is the **XLS-R + AASIST** checkpoint (MIT; EER 0.82% on ASVspoof 2021 LA, 2.85% DF), English-trained ([GitHub TakHemlata/SSL_Anti-spoofing](https://github.com/TakHemlata/SSL_Anti-spoofing)). None of the public spoof sets other than ASVspoof 2021's compression conditions models a telephone channel, so passing bona-fide and spoofed Assamese through an 8 kHz codec chain is part of the recipe, not an optional extra.

Priority for E3: first, accept the IndicVoices gate, verify the per-row speaker-ID schema and cross-task consistency, and fine-tune or at least score-calibrate the encoders on Assamese 8 kHz conversational speech with speaker-disjoint trials; second, add VAANI Assam as a same-region cohort and Nagamese/Karbi as non-Assamese impostors; third, build "AS-Spoof" from IndicVoices bona-fide speakers using Parler-TTS, IndicF5 and FreeVC, codec-degrade to 8 kHz, and fine-tune XLS-R-AASIST, with IndicSynth Bengali as non-commercial pre-training only; fourth, request LDC-IL for dialect profiling and register for I-MSV if multi-session data is needed. Genuine multi-session Assamese telephone recordings of known speakers do not exist publicly and must be recorded.

## E4: Broaden the machine class before touching the benchmark question

The project cannot validate against any existing MGT benchmark because none includes Assamese or Bengali. **M4GT-Bench** covers Arabic, Bulgarian, Chinese, Indonesian, Russian, Urdu, Italian and German ([arXiv 2402.11175 HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.11175v2)); **SemEval-2024 Task 8** adds Urdu only as 2,899 ChatGPT plus 3,000 human news texts ([arXiv 2404.14183 HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.14183)); the **COLING-2025 GenAI Detection Task 1** includes Urdu in training and a **600/600 Hindi test slice**, on which the best system reached **only 51.8** while the Urdu best was 68.7 ([arXiv 2501.11012 HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.11012v2)). M4GT's held-out-language results (Bulgarian 52.7%, Russian 53.7%, Indonesian 55.8%) tell the same story: cross-lingual and cross-genre transfer collapses to chance, which independently supports the project's choice of unlabelled target-domain adaptation. The COLING data is on Hugging Face without a licence tag ([HF Jinyan1/COLING_2025_MGT_multingual](https://huggingface.co/datasets/Jinyan1/COLING_2025_MGT_multingual)); its Urdu and Hindi news slices are worth using only as a cross-lingual sanity check, not as training data.

What does exist in Assamese is machine-*translated* text at enormous scale, and the current detector has never seen it. **Sangraha Synthetic** is **11,696.4M Assamese tokens** of English Wikimedia translated by IndicTrans2 and then romanised with IndicXlit, released CC BY 4.0 without quality filtering, as `asm_Beng` and `asm_Latn` splits of ~5.63M rows each ([HF ai4bharat/sangraha](https://huggingface.co/datasets/ai4bharat/sangraha); [arXiv 2403.06350 HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.06350)). Because it is parallel to Wikipedia, it is genre-matched to the project's human Wikipedia negatives — a controlled pair that isolates translation artefacts from topic. **IndicAlign** (CC BY 4.0, ~97.4M rows) contributes Llama2-70B-Chat responses generated in English and IndicTrans2-translated into Assamese across instruction, conversation and refusal subsets ([HF ai4bharat/indic-align](https://huggingface.co/datasets/ai4bharat/indic-align)). The Alpaca-style Assamese sets (`Telugu-LLM-Labs`, 28,910 rows; `Tensoic/GPTeacher-Assamese`, 43,654 rows) have no licence and undocumented translation models ([HF](https://huggingface.co/datasets/Telugu-LLM-Labs/assamese_alpaca_yahma_cleaned_filtered); [HF](https://huggingface.co/datasets/Tensoic/GPTeacher-Assamese)); `ananddey/assamese-sft-dataset-v1` (61,929 rows, Apache-2.0) is a cleaned aggregate that already shows translation artefacts such as stray spaces around apostrophes ([HF](https://huggingface.co/datasets/ananddey/assamese-sft-dataset-v1)). The project's current machine source, `indian-nlp/assamese-dataset`, is MIT-licensed on GitHub but its paper describes only **"10,000 lines"** of ChatGPT 3.5 word-prompted output ([GitHub indian-nlp/assamese-dataset](https://github.com/indian-nlp/assamese-dataset); [arXiv 2410.11291 HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.11291v2)), and the HF mirror returns 401 ([HF API](https://huggingface.co/api/datasets/indian-nlp/assamese-dataset)). Aya has no Assamese split at all ([HF datasets-server splits](https://datasets-server.huggingface.co/splits?dataset=CohereLabs/aya_collection_language_split)).

A native multi-generator set has to be produced locally, and the generator shortlist is short. **Qwen3** names Assamese among its 119 supported languages and Qwen3-32B is Apache-2.0 ([Qwen3 blog](https://qwenlm.github.io/blog/qwen3/)); **Krutrim-2-instruct** tags `as` under a custom community licence ([model card](https://huggingface.co/krutrim-ai-labs/Krutrim-2-instruct)); **BLOOMZ** lists `as` ([HF API bigscience/bloomz](https://huggingface.co/api/models/bigscience/bloomz)). **Sarvam-M** excludes Assamese from its ten Indic languages ([Sarvam blog](https://www.sarvam.ai/blogs/sarvam-m)), Llama 3.1 and Aya Expanse do not cover it, and no published Assamese-specific generation-quality scores for Gemma 3, GPT-4o, Claude or Gemini were found. Tokenizer efficiency for Assamese ranks **SUTRA 0.45 < GPT-4o 0.54 < Gemma 2 0.82 < Llama 3.1 1.4 < Mistral 1.48** (normalised sequence length, lower is better) ([arXiv 2410.03718](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.03718)), a rough proxy for which models will produce fluent rather than fragmented output. **IndicGenBench** (Google, Assamese included; CrossSum-IN and Flores-IN under CC BY-SA/CC BY-NC-SA) supplies human Assamese references parallel to English sources, so prompt-matched human/machine pairs can be generated from the same English input ([GitHub](https://github.com/google-research-datasets/indic-gen-bench)). Expect Bengali-script contamination (র for ৰ, missing ৱ) in weaker generators: it is both a cheap tell and a confound that fires on human Bengali-influenced or OCR'd text, and no quantitative study of these substitution rates exists ([HF promptmafiainc](https://huggingface.co/datasets/promptmafiainc/assamese-bengali-contrastive-eval)).

Human genre coverage is uneven. News and web are abundant (IndicCorp v2 3.38M lines CC0; Sangraha-verified 292.1M tokens, of which 162.9M are OCR'd PDFs) ([HF ai4bharat/IndicCorpV2](https://huggingface.co/datasets/ai4bharat/IndicCorpV2); [HF ai4bharat/sangraha](https://huggingface.co/datasets/ai4bharat/sangraha)). Literature is served by **Xahitya: 9,430 articles (prose, essays, stories, poems, 2011–2026)** with URL, title and date fields, tagged CC0 while acknowledging author rights ([HF](https://huggingface.co/datasets/Ranjit89/xahitya-assamese-corpus)) — a substantial upgrade on the Arambhani scrape. Conversational Assamese comes from **IndicVoices transcripts**: role-play telephone dialogues and extempore answers, ~106 transcribed Assamese hours, gated CC BY 4.0 ([arXiv 2403.01926 HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01926)). Social media is the thin genre: **HASOC 2023 Assamese** (Twitter/Facebook/YouTube comments, binary hate labels) is password-protected behind a Google Form with no published counts ([task site](https://sites.google.com/view/hasoc-2023-annihilate-hates)), and the 8,986-review movie-sentiment set is **~60% machine-translated or normalised** and includes Bengali rows, so it must not serve as "human Assamese" ([HF](https://huggingface.co/datasets/AvinabhDutta-Dev/assamese-movie-reviews-sentiment)). No dedicated legal or government Assamese corpus was found; Samanantar's `data_source` field (anuvaad_pib, pmi, newsonair; CC BY-NC-4.0) is the only labelled proxy ([HF ai4bharat/samanantar](https://huggingface.co/datasets/ai4bharat/samanantar)). Most monolingual corpora carry no genre tag, so genre-stratified evaluation means reconstructing genre from source.

Priority for E4: first, add Sangraha Synthetic `asm_Beng` as a machine-translation class paired against Wikipedia and measure whether the current detector sees it at all; second, generate a native multi-generator set with Qwen3, Krutrim-2 and API models from IndicGenBench English sources and from Xahitya/IndicCorp seeds, paraphrase-style, so every machine text has a human twin; third, stratify human negatives by genre using IndicCorp v2 (news), Xahitya (literature), IndicVoices transcripts (conversational) and HASOC Assamese (social, by request); fourth, treat ৰ/ৱ frequency as an explicit audited feature rather than letting it drive the classifier silently. A released prompt-matched Assamese MGT benchmark would be the first of its kind.

## What the researchers could not verify

The notes were compiled without a general web-search tool, through hub APIs, arXiv, Crossref/OpenAlex and direct fetches; Semantic Scholar rate-limited, dblp and Mojeek blocked, and several publisher pages returned 403 or 404. The unverified items that most affect the recommendations are these. The two Springer chapters on Assamese authorship (and the 9,730-sample figure) were not opened, so AAALC's identity with that corpus is inferred. The IIT Guwahati OSINTG resources page is dead, so the group's larger Facebook/X/YouTube holdings and any request process are unknown. The OSINTG repository licence has not been posted. IndicVoices' per-row metadata schema, current Assamese hours and cross-task speaker-ID consistency are not on the card; VAANI's sampling rate, device and per-row fields are undocumented; LDC-IL prices and undertaking texts are login-gated; InfoBay pricing is undisclosed. Common Voice Assamese could not be inventoried on the Mozilla Data Collective. HASOC 2023 Assamese counts live only in an unfetched ACM overview, and the "5,044-comment Assamese hate-speech corpus" and the Bodoland University dialect dataset could not be located. RomanSetu's six languages, I-MSV 2022's language list and access route, CTS Superset's language list, SEA-Spoof and HAV-DF release locations, and BanglaTLit's data licence are all unconfirmed. The author-conditioned synthetic-augmentation literature for low-resource authorship was effectively unresearched rather than found empty. PAN, FIRE/ICON code-mixed shared tasks, Kaggle, Shaip/Defined.ai/Appen catalogues and Bhashini/ULCA model licences were not reached.

## Conclusion

The survey reframes the binding constraint. The project is not short of Assamese; it is short of Assamese with forensic metadata, and the one place that metadata exists at scale is the AI4Bharat and IISc speech programme. IndicVoices alone converts E3 from a zero-shot demonstration into a trainable, calibratable system with speaker-disjoint trials on genuine 8 kHz Assamese, and the same corpus feeds E4 with conversational human text and E3's spoof construction with bona-fide speakers. For text, the honest conclusion is that author identity in informal Assamese has been stripped from every public release for exactly the privacy reasons that make forensic data hard to publish, so E1 either accepts the Wikipedia register compromise or funds consented collection; there is no third path in the public domain.

Two implications follow for sequencing. The gated-but-free tier should be cleared this week — accept the IndicVoices, VAANI and IndicF5 gates, download Aksharantar, Sangraha, IndicCorp v2, AAALC and the aswiki stub history — because every later decision (whether speaker IDs are consistent across channels, how many Wikipedia editors have enough text, how badly the detector misses machine translation) depends on facts that only inspection of those files can settle. And the project should move its own licensing house into order: the AssameseBackTranslit repository that underpins E2 has no licence, and any published artefact derived from it inherits that gap until IIT Guwahati posts the terms it promised.
